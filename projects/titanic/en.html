The problem

James Cameron’s Titanic brought the voyage that began in Southampton on 10 April 1912 to cinema audiences worldwide. Which passenger characteristics were associated with survival?

The approach

I explored 891 passenger records in Google Colab using Python and Pandas. I compared age, sex and ticket class, then trained logistic regression and decision tree models using those three features.

Good practices checklist

  • I set aside 179 passengers for testing and used 712 for training, preserving the proportion of survivors in each set.
  • For the charts I used the 714 recorded ages. The training pipeline filled missing ages with the training median and encoded sex numerically.
  • I compared both models across the same five training folds.
  • I compared the final predictions with a majority-class baseline and counted the survivors the tree missed.

Results and visualization

Passenger ages

Age and survival

Among passengers with recorded ages, 57.4% of those under 12 survived, compared with 38.9% of those aged 12 or older. This cutoff was used for exploration. The tree learned its own age thresholds.

How well did the tree perform?

Mean cross-validation accuracy was 81.2% for the tree and 78.8% for logistic regression. On the test set the tree classified 137 of 179 passengers correctly (76.5%). Always predicting death would yield 110 correct predictions (61.5%).

Where does the estimate come from?

The tree asks up to three questions to reach a group of training passengers. For a 38-year-old woman travelling first class, 127 of 133 passengers in her group survived (95.5%). Some groups are small and the dataset covers only part of the voyage’s circumstances.

Each simulated voyage draws an outcome using the model estimate. The lucky number and zodiac sign adjust the odds as fictional game rules. The performance figures above evaluate the original tree.
